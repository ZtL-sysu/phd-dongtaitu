Event Driven Spiking Dynamic Graph Networks for 
Nonstationary Temporal Graph Forecasting  
Doctoral Research Proposal  
Abstract 
Digital interactions generate evolving networks in which predictive relationships change over time. 
Communication patterns shift between groups, learners redistribute their attention across online course activities, 
and trading relationships evolve between economies. Forecasting these processes requires models to revise 
relationships, retain useful historical information, and allocate computation selectively. Updating every 
relationship and hidden state at every observation can be wasteful; reusing them indiscriminately can leave a 
model poorly prepared for abrupt changes. 
This research will investigate event-driven spiking dynamic graph networks for nonstationary temporal graph 
forecasting. It will develop sparse relationship learning that distinguishes credible changes from noise and 
missing records, relation-conditioned spiking memory, and a lightweight scheduler for budgeted graph and state 
updates. The central hypothesis is that coordinating these components improves adaptation and the accuracy–cost 
trade-off. The project combines conditional approximation analysis, controlled simulations, and substantial 
empirical evaluation using public datasets. Communication and online-learning records will support multistep 
activity forecasting, while international trade will test future relationship distributions. Recommendation and 
transaction networks will extend application coverage using their native prediction protocols. Evaluation will 
isolate graph adaptation, spiking dynamics, and event gating, and measure complete software execution cost. The 
intended outcome is a reproducible data science methodology explaining when selective computation improves 
prediction and when it fails, supported by cross-domain case studies on historical data. 
1 Research Background and Significance 
Many data science problems concern interacting entities rather than isolated time series. Users, learning activities, 
products, and countries can be represented as nodes, with timestamped interactions providing observed edges. 
Graph forecasting can then predict future activity levels or the distribution of future relationships. An observed 
interaction and a useful predictive dependency are distinct: a historical contact can inform a prior, while a learned 
edge expresses how one entity's past contributes to another entity's forecast. In this proposal, relational structure 
takes the role often called spatial dependence in spatiotemporal models; it need not describe geographic 
proximity. 
This distinction becomes important in nonstationary environments. Nonstationarity here means that the 
distribution of observations or their predictive dependencies changes over time, so patterns learned from earlier 
periods may become less reliable. It includes abrupt regime transitions, gradual changes, and recurring regimes, 
consistent with the broader concept-drift literature [1]. Ordinary fluctuations and measurement noise do not 
necessarily require structural adaptation. A useful forecasting model must identify changes that matter for 
prediction while avoiding unnecessary responses to harmless variation. 
The difficulty involves memory as well as spatial structure. When an influential neighbor changes, historical 
information accumulated from that neighbor can become less useful. Nevertheless, deleting all history may 
remove stable seasonal information or slowly varying system conditions. This creates a selective retention 
problem: the model must preserve background context while adapting the parts of its state associated with 
changed dependencies. A forecasting architecture that updates its graph without reconsidering the meaning of 
stored information may therefore adapt incompletely. 
Computation introduces a further constraint. Streaming systems require repeated forecasts even when only a 
small part of the monitored network changes. The number of possible pairwise relationships grows quadratically 
with the number of nodes, and repeated state updates can add substantial cost. Spiking neural networks offer

The doctoral project is positioned within data science, combining methodological and theoretical research with

scheduling, while each task retains its own output head and evaluation protocol. Empirical case studies will 
establish practical relevance in communication analysis, online education, trade, recommendation, and 
transaction networks. The research will explain both favorable and unfavorable conditions for adaptation under 
computational constraints. 
2 Related Research and the Proposed Research Gap 
2.1 Graph methods for spatiotemporal forecasting 
DCRNN combines diffusion on a directed graph with recurrent sequence modeling and provides an established 
traffic forecasting formulation [2]. Graph WaveNet introduces an adaptive dependency matrix together with 
dilated temporal convolutions [3], while AGCRN learns node-specific patterns and data-adaptive graph 
relationships [4]. These methods show why forecasting can benefit from relations beyond a predefined physical 
graph. However, learning an adjacency matrix during training does not necessarily mean that relationships are 
updated in response to each new observation during inference. 
DGCRN explicitly generates time-dependent graph structure from evolving node information and combines it 
with predefined relations [5]. It is therefore a necessary comparison for a proposal concerned with changing 
predictive dependencies. The proposed project must establish a benefit beyond the general idea of a dynamic 
adjacency matrix. Specifically, it will examine whether selective relation updates and coordinated memory 
adjustment improve adaptation at a comparable computational cost. 
Complex graph architectures also need comparisons with efficient alternatives. STID demonstrates the strength of 
relatively simple forecasting models supplied with spatial and temporal identity information [6]. STAEformer 
shows the importance of suitable spatiotemporal embeddings within a Transformer [7]. These findings motivate a 
baseline-first approach: performance gains must survive comparisons with strong, simpler models and cannot be 
attributed automatically to architectural complexity. A selective state-space approach such as STG-Mamba will 
provide an additional efficiency-oriented comparison when a compatible implementation is available [8]. 
2.2 Spiking networks for dynamic graphs and forecasting 
Surrogate-gradient learning provides a practical route for training networks with discrete spike events [9]. 
SpikeNet already uses spiking neurons for dynamic graph representation learning [10]. Delay-DSGN introduces 
learnable delays to model how historical information affects evolving graph representations [11]. ChronoSpike 
further explores adaptive membrane dynamics, spatial aggregation, and temporal encoding [12]. These studies 
rule out a broad novelty claim based simply on applying spiking neural networks to dynamic graphs or adding 
adjustable temporal memory. 
Forecasting is also represented in the recent literature. SpikeSTAG combines graph learning with spiking 
temporal processing for multivariate prediction [13]. SpikySpace investigates a spiking state-space formulation 
with selective temporal computation [14]. Both will be considered when assessing the contribution of the 
proposed method. The cited versions of ChronoSpike, SpikeSTAG, SpikySpace, and STG-Mamba are preprints, 
and their methodological claims will be assessed alongside established publications. Reported classification 
results from graph representation studies will not be compared directly with forecasting errors. 
The literature therefore motivates a narrower research question: can evidence of changing predictive relationships 
jointly determine memory retention and computation refresh? Temporal Graph Networks provide an important

2.3 Missing observations and event-triggered computation 
Missingness is relevant because incomplete event records can resemble a change in behavior. GRU-D 
demonstrates the usefulness of observation masks and elapsed-time information in recurrent modeling [15]. This 
project will incorporate comparable information into graph learning and update decisions. A zero event count 
within a fully observed interval will remain distinct from an unavailable record. Robustness tests will simulate 
missing time blocks and incomplete groups of entity histories without treating every absent interaction as missing 
data. 
Event-triggered control provides a conceptual precedent for executing an update when an error-related condition 
requires it [16]. Its stability results do not transfer automatically to learned forecasting networks. Here, the 
connection will guide the design of lightweight triggers and explicit approximation analysis. The research gap is 
the coordinated treatment of relation revision, memory adaptation, and selective execution in the chosen 
forecasting setting. Whether this combination provides a distinct and useful contribution remains an empirical 
question, to be reassessed as the literature develops. 
3 Research Aim Questions and Hypotheses 
The overall aim is to develop and evaluate a forecasting framework that adapts to changing dependencies among 
interacting entities while allocating computation according to current predictive need. The research will address 
three questions, each with a mechanism-level hypothesis and a condition under which it would be rejected. 
RQ1 asks how sparse predictive relationships can be updated without confusing noisy or incomplete records with 
structural change. Hypothesis H1 is that confidence-aware graph updates, conditioned on observation masks and 
elapsed time, reduce unnecessary edge variation while remaining responsive to genuine dependency changes. 
This hypothesis will be weakened if matched static or periodically updated graphs perform equally well under 
controlled relation shifts, or if the proposed learner changes edges mainly when inputs are corrupted. 
RQ2 asks how historical memory should respond when predictive relationships change. Hypothesis H2 is that 
conditioning memory retention on local relation changes will improve recovery after regime transitions compared 
with memory and graph adaptation learned independently. Evidence must persist against parameter-matched 
models with the same fast and slow states. If uncoupled memory performs equally well, or if improvements 
disappear after controlling for capacity and lookback length, the proposed coordination mechanism will not be 
supported. 
RQ3 asks whether graph and state updates can be scheduled within a budget while keeping the additional 
prediction error acceptable. Hypothesis H3 is that lightweight local triggers with bounded state age will provide a 
better accuracy–cost trade-off than periodic or random updates with comparable budgets. The hypothesis fails if 
trigger evaluation and sparse execution remove the expected savings, or if selective updates systematically miss 
important transitions. The project will also determine whether any benefit depends on spiking dynamics by 
applying comparable gates to a continuous-valued temporal model. 
These questions define related but independently assessable contributions. A positive result in one component 
will not be treated as evidence for all three. The thesis can therefore distinguish useful mechanisms from 
unnecessary architectural additions and explain where their interaction is beneficial.

4.1 Forecasting formulation and information availability 
Let Xt denote observed activity features at N entities, Mt the observation mask, and Dt elapsed time 
since the last valid observation. Given a history of length L and an optional relation prior Aprior, the 
model will predict H future feature vectors. Its information set contains only records, metadata, and 
states available at forecast origin t. Calendar features may be used only when timestamps retain their 
calendar meaning. Future realized interactions, full-period graph statistics, and retrospectively assigned 
labels are excluded from inputs. For native ranking tasks, the same causal encoder will feed a separate 
distribution or pair-scoring head, as specified in Section 5.6.

4.2 Work package one on sparse changing relationships 
The first work package will construct sparse predictive graphs from historical interaction priors and 
bounded candidate neighborhoods. Candidates may include past communication partners, entities 
sharing earlier interaction histories, and additional relations identified from training observations. Only 
past edges may enter a prior at a given prediction origin. A less frequent exploration step will allow 
previously excluded candidates to enter, reducing the risk that a permanently restricted neighborhood

For each candidate edge, a lightweight scoring function will combine recent node summaries, relative 
observation age, and missingness indicators. A sparse selection operation will retain a bounded number 
of neighbors. The training objective will penalize unnecessary changes in adjacency, with weaker 
temporal smoothing when credible evidence of a transition is present. Confidence will be estimated 
from currently available residuals and observation quality, rather than from future forecast errors. A 
robust normalization rule will prevent a single corrupted measurement from dominating the change

Confidence will initially be computed from the proportion of valid recent observations and a clipped

constant confidence will isolate the value of reliability weighting; equal candidate sets and exploration 
schedules will prevent a larger search space from explaining the result. 
The main mechanism experiment will use a switching dynamical system with known dependency 
matrices. It will vary edge changes independently of observation noise, allowing measurement of 
structural tracking delay and false changes. Recovery of the generating structure will be interpreted 
only under explicitly stated identifiability conditions. On real communication, learning, and trade 
records, graph visualizations will instead serve as diagnostics of learned predictive associations and will 
be checked against out-of-time predictive performance.

only two timescales to make the mechanism interpretable and avoid unnecessary capacity. A 
continuous-valued readout will map the resulting state representation to real-valued multistep

Each fast or slow state will partition a fixed-width representation into local and neighbor-dependent 
channels. The multiplier will act only on the neighbor-dependent channels; elapsed-time decay remains 
active in both. If graph refresh occurs before an expensive state update, the multiplier will be stored and 
accumulated until that update, with its bookkeeping included in cost. The initial implementation will 
use a shared positive sensitivity, followed by a node-conditioned version only if justified. A shuffled

comes from informative coordination rather than generic forgetting. Controls will match total state 
width, not merely the number of named state modules. 
Input encoding will preserve both absolute levels and changes. A purely difference-based code could 
become silent while a variable remains at a high, predictively important level. I will compare change-
only encoding, periodic level refresh, and a hybrid level-and-change representation. The continuous 
pathway will be counted as part of the model's computational cost. Its inclusion will be justified by 
measured predictive value rather than hidden behind a claim of entirely spike-based processing. 
Decisive ablations will remove relation-conditioned retention, freeze decay constants, replace spiking 
states with continuous recurrent units, and preserve the same graph learner throughout. A parameter-
matched uncoupled model will test whether gains come from coordination rather than additional state 
dimensions. Recovery curves after known dependency switches will provide a more direct assessment 
than average forecasting error alone. 
4.4 Work package three on budgeted event-triggered execution 
The third work package will decide when graph rows and neural states require expensive updates. At 
each observation time, a small monitoring computation will inspect new measurements, masks, cached 
predictions, and state age. A residual at time t can compare Xt with the one-step prediction issued at t-1; 
it cannot use the unknown target at t+1. Separate gates will govern graph revision and state refresh, 
allowing a state update to occur without reconstructing its neighborhood. 
Initial triggers will be deterministic threshold rules, establishing an interpretable baseline before any 
learned scheduler is introduced. Nodes with large reliable residuals, stale states, or substantial 
observation-quality changes will receive higher priority. When several connected nodes trigger, their 
updates can be grouped to reduce execution overhead. A maximum permitted age and periodic audits

The primary budget B will constrain average execution cost, with a separately reported per-step latency 
requirement. If many nodes require updates simultaneously, the system may use a bounded full-refresh 
mode and record the resulting budget overrun. An average budget does not establish a hard worst-case 
guarantee. A later hard-budget variant will prioritize mandatory updates and state explicitly when both 
refresh requirements and the budget cannot be satisfied. 
Skipping an expensive update will still require coherent time evolution. Cached states will undergo 
analytically computed decay where valid, with cheap monitoring information retained for future 
decisions. Applying a zero mask after executing the full graph will not count as saved computation. The 
implementation must avoid the associated operations through indexed execution, grouped active 
neighborhoods, or another verifiable sparse path. A direct forecast readout will still run at every 
required prediction origin, and its cost will be included. 
Scheduler learning, if needed, will begin from the validated full-update model and use computation 
penalties or constrained optimization. Training and inference costs will be reported separately. The 
scheduler will also be applied to a matched nonspiking model. This comparison will establish whether 
the advantage is attributable to event scheduling in general, the temporal state representation, or their 
interaction. 
The scheduler must not compute the expensive new graph merely to decide whether that graph should 
be computed. Before refresh, it will use cheap activity summaries, already available residuals, and time 
since the last graph or state update. The exact adjacency-change score will be computed only after an 
authorized graph refresh and used for retention. Eligible actions will first include maximum-age 
refreshes, followed by optional actions ranked by estimated benefit per unit cost. Measured action costs 
will be calibrated on validation replay. If mandatory actions exceed the nominal budget, the excess will 
be logged rather than hidden by dropping difficult queries. Graph and state budgets will also be swept 
independently to reveal which action accounts for the trade-off. 
4.5 Approximation analysis and its limits 
The theoretical objective is to characterize the difference between selective and full execution of the 
same trained model under identical observations and initialization. This execution-error analysis is 
distinct from statistical generalization or the accuracy of the forecast itself. Under bounded inputs and 
graph operators, and within regions where spike decisions agree, I will investigate the following 
recurrence for state discrepancy et. 
𝑒𝑡  ≤  𝜌𝑒𝑡−1  +  𝑐𝐴𝛿𝐴(𝑡) + 𝑐𝐼𝛿𝐼(𝑡) +  𝑐𝑆𝑞𝑡 
Here, δA and δI describe differences caused by stale graph information and omitted input updates, while 
qt accounts for mismatched spike events. Constants and norms will be derived for the implemented 
update equations. A contraction-based argument will require the complete state transition to satisfy the 
relevant bound; membrane leakage alone will not establish this property. A Lipschitz readout can then 
translate state discrepancy into a bound on disagreement between predictions. 
Threshold discontinuities are a substantive difficulty. If membrane potentials remain sufficiently far 
from their thresholds, small perturbations may preserve spike decisions; near a threshold, this margin 
argument fails. The analysis will therefore distinguish conditional deterministic bounds from empirical 
behavior and investigate conservative refreshes near threshold crossings. With bounded perturbations 
and rho below one, the recurrence suggests how discrepancy may be controlled. If these assumptions

For the simplified fixed-node system, the analysis will first bound one graph-aggregation step and one

gives a weighted sum of past graph, input, and spike-decision errors; a time-uniform bound additionally 
requires a contraction condition and bounded disturbance terms. Numerical diagnostics will estimate 
local sensitivity and the frequency of threshold-margin violations on held-out inputs. These diagnostics 
test the plausibility of the assumptions but do not replace a proof. The theory deliverable will state 
which operators, parameter constraints, and time horizon are covered, alongside counterexamples or 
observed violations.

5 Experimental Design and Evaluation 
5.1 Data and controlled mechanisms 
The primary suite will use public communication, online-education, and trade records. SNAP email-Eu-
core-temporal supports daily incoming and outgoing activity prediction [18]; SNAP MOOC actions 
support course-activity count forecasting [19]; and TGB tgbn-trade supports next-year trade-partner 
distribution prediction [17,20]. Initial count-forecasting protocols will use 28 preceding daily bins for 
email and 24 preceding hourly bins for MOOC, predicting respectively 1, 3, and 7 days and 1, 6, and 24 
hours ahead. These aggregation choices are proposed research protocols, not official leaderboard tasks. 
The native trade protocol will retain its annual resolution and official evaluation. Dataset versions, 
temporal coverage, terms of use, and preprocessing will be recorded before baseline runs. 
A complementary synthetic benchmark will use stable sparse vector autoregressive dynamics with 
controlled regime changes. Simulations will separately manipulate edge weights, neighbor identities, 
noise levels, local shocks, and gradual drift. Stability checks and bounded trajectories will prevent 
artificial divergence from dominating the task. Additional nonlinear simulations will test whether 
conclusions depend on matching the model to a linear generator. Known structural changes provide 
ground truth for detection delay; ordinary shocks without structural changes provide a negative control. 
The controlled study will begin with 64-node systems, extending to 256 nodes for scaling checks. 
Stable sparse transition matrices will be rescaled to a common spectral-radius target of 0.8, with 
bounded innovation variance and a discarded burn-in. Abrupt changes will replace 10 or 30 percent of 
active dependencies; gradual changes will interpolate between stable endpoints while checking 
intermediate stability. Noise-only shocks and missing-input rates of 0, 10, 30, and 50 percent provide 
separate controls. Changing edge identities and changing edge weights will be tested separately. 
Parameters, switch times, and random seeds will be released, and held-out simulations will use new 
trajectories rather than reused training segments. 
Recommendation and transaction data will broaden empirical coverage through tgbn-genre and tgbl-
coin, with tgbl-wiki-v2 providing a smaller event-ranking development benchmark [20,21]. The 
primary three-domain evaluation is a scheduled thesis deliverable; the larger extensions follow a 
measured feasibility gate rather than replacing unfinished core experiments. Section 5.6 specifies entity 
definitions, output targets, application questions, and limits for each domain. All real-world analyses 
will use released historical datasets and reproducible chronological replay. The resulting application 
evidence will concern predictive performance on recorded behavior, with prospective organizational 
impact left as a separate question.

5.2 Temporal protocols and leakage prevention 
Custom activity-forecasting tasks will use chronological 70/10/20 percent train/validation/test partitions, 
recording exact boundaries and requiring complete target windows within each partition. Native TGB 
tasks will retain the released splits, label availability, and evaluation schedule. Earlier records may 
supply causal context, but normalization, activity thresholds, graph candidates, and entity selection 
must not use later observations. The core fixed-entity cohort will be defined from training information; 
subsequent arrivals will enter a separately reported induction study. Zero counts will be assigned only 
to intervals treated as fully observed; unavailable inputs will carry masks and unavailable targets will be 
excluded consistently. 
The primary window-based protocol will initialize each model using the same permitted history, with 
selective state reuse occurring within that history. A second streaming protocol will process 
observations once in chronological order and preserve state across forecast origins. This distinction 
matters because persistent state provides a different effective history and cost profile. Methods will be 
compared within each protocol; efficiency claims will identify which protocol they concern. Test states 
will be initialized through a prescribed causal warm-up, and no future sequence will be processed in 
advance. 
Nonstationary evaluation will include controlled interventions, later-period performance, and 
predefined high-change slices. On real data, changes identified from past activity or interaction 
distributions will be treated as observable proxies rather than verified causal regime labels. Thresholds 
will be set on training and validation data. Separate within-domain tests will examine low-activity 
entities, new partners, and long inactive gaps. Cross-domain evidence will mean independently trained 
evaluations under a common methodological protocol; any claim of direct model transfer will require 
an additional experiment with explicit feature alignment and disjoint entities. 
Figure 2 distinguishes two causal evaluation orders. In binned forecasting, a completed input interval 
may update the model before future intervals are predicted. In event ranking, the current destination

5.3 Baselines and matched comparisons 
Activity-forecasting baselines will include persistence, seasonal historical averages where periodicity is 
justified, a linear predictor, and a compact recurrent model. Graph WaveNet, AGCRN, DGCRN, STID, 
and STAEformer [3–7] will be adapted to the same count features and horizons; DCRNN [2] will use a 
training-period interaction prior. These are adaptations from related forecasting settings, whose original 
reported scores are not comparable to the proposed tasks. Native ranking comparisons will include 
historical-frequency and recency baselines, TGB reference methods, TGN, and DyGFormer [17,22,23], 
with matching output heads and official evaluators. Recent state-space and spiking methods [8,13,14] 
will be assessed for compatible reruns. A maintained literature review will refresh strong task-specific 
alternatives before the main studies. 
SpikeNet, Delay-DSGN, and ChronoSpike [10–12] will serve as method-family context and potential 
adaptation studies. Their original node-classification tasks require a forecasting head and an appropriate 
input protocol before quantitative comparison. If adaptation is impractical, they will remain literature 
comparisons with an explicit reason. Published scores from incompatible splits, preprocessing, or tasks 
will not be mixed into a rerun results table. 
Internal controls will cross graph type, temporal representation, and update policy. Important 
comparisons are static versus dynamic graphs with the same temporal module; spiking versus 
continuous states with the same graph learner; full versus selective updates within the same model; and 
matched gating on both spiking and nonspiking networks. Periodic and random schedules will use 
comparable realized budgets. An offline oracle with privileged change information may be used only as 
a labeled upper-bound diagnostic on synthetic data, never as a deployable method.

5.4 Metrics statistical evidence and decision rules 
For count forecasts, primary accuracy measures will be mean absolute error and root mean squared 
error in original count units, reported separately by domain, horizon, and activity stratum. Nonnegative 
outputs and a count-appropriate loss will be compared with ordinary squared-error training; any log 
transform will be inverted for primary scoring. Native node-distribution tasks will use NDCG@10, and 
native link ranking will use MRR with the specified candidate protocol [20,21]. Scores from these task 
families will remain separate. Transition-window degradation, recovery after controlled changes, and 
error–runtime Pareto curves will connect accuracy to the adaptation hypotheses. 
Robustness experiments will remove selected observed input records, contiguous time blocks, or 
histories of related entity groups while preserving known targets for evaluation. Additional 
perturbations will vary event timing and count noise within a documented range. All methods will 
receive identical perturbations, with training augmentation separated from unseen test corruption. For 
ranking tasks, history corruption will not silently change the official evaluation candidates or labels. 
Noise-only experiments will measure false updates; relationship-change experiments will measure 
missed or delayed responses. These tests will show whether savings arise from useful selectivity or 
from neglecting difficult observations. 
Each principal comparison will use five random seeds, with paired evaluation on the same splits and 
perturbations. Confidence intervals will account for temporal dependence through block resampling of 
forecast errors, with block length selected using training or validation dependence diagnostics. Seed 
variation will be reported separately from uncertainty across time periods. Predefined primary

comparisons will reduce selective emphasis on favorable horizons, and any broad multiple-comparison 
analysis will report an appropriate correction or remain explicitly exploratory. 
Success criteria will be calibrated after baseline reproduction and before final testing. The minimum 
criterion for selective execution will be a validated accuracy tolerance accompanied by a measurable 
reduction in total inference cost. Strong evidence will require a reproducible Pareto improvement 
against both full execution and matched alternative schedules across the primary datasets, without 
unacceptable transition degradation. The tolerance will be set using baseline variability and application 
relevance rather than a desired headline percentage. If only operation estimates improve, the conclusion 
will be restricted to algorithmic sparsity.

promised gains. Ranking tasks will retain their native score direction and report absolute score 
differences. The primary acceptable tolerance will be justified and frozen using validation evidence

5.5 Efficiency measurement and reproducibility 
Cost accounting will cover input encoding, candidate search, graph scoring, message propagation, 
neural-state updates, monitoring, scheduling, and prediction readout. Measurements will include batch-

one end-to-end latency, throughput where relevant, peak memory, graph-row update fraction, state 
refresh fraction, and spike activity. Warm-up runs, device synchronization, precision, and batch settings 
will be standardized. CPU and GPU results will be distinguished because sparse workloads may have 
different overheads on each platform. 
Efficiency evidence will be based on reproducible software experiments: end-to-end runtime, peak 
memory, processed events, and graph/state update counts. Operation counts will be reported as 
algorithmic proxies alongside measured execution time. All local code will run in a documented Conda 
environment, retaining package versions, seeds, data manifests, configurations, checkpoints, and 
evaluation scripts. Dataset preparation and inference will be timed separately. The final package will 
expose a common interface for chronological replay across domains and include unsuccessful ablations 
that clarify the method's limits. 
The reproducibility package will include a small smoke-test dataset, a single-command baseline run, 
explicit configuration files, and a replay trace with prediction origin, input cutoff, state age, graph-
refresh decision, state-refresh decision, and measured duration. Basic checks will verify causal feature 
construction, equivalence of selective and full code paths when all gates are open, correct reuse when 
gates are closed, and official-evaluator agreement. Runtime trials will repeat the same replay at least 
five times after an explicitly stated warm-up, reporting median and tail latency as well as total elapsed 
time. Compilation, data loading, and preprocessing will be itemized so that their inclusion is consistent 
across methods. 
5.6 Cross-domain application studies on public data 
Communication analysis will use email-Eu-core-temporal, where directed timestamped messages 
connect anonymized members of a research institution [18]. The proposed task aggregates incoming 
and outgoing counts into daily entity features and learns changing predictive relationships from past 
communication. Case studies will examine whether selective updates preserve forecasts during bursts 
and shifting interaction patterns while avoiding excessive computation during quiet periods. Evaluation 
will report errors by activity level and forecast horizon, as well as aggregate workload trajectories. The 
dataset covers internal communication; conclusions will therefore concern the recorded network rather 
than all organizational activity. Since timestamps are relative, periodic lags can be tested without 
assigning unsupported calendar dates. 
Online-education analysis will use the public MOOC action log, whose nodes represent users and 
course activities and whose edges are timestamped actions [19]. The core task predicts future hourly 
action counts for course-activity nodes. A rolling shared-user history will provide an optional relation 
prior between activities, constructed solely from earlier actions. The study will compare stable 
engagement periods with abrupt changes in activity demand and assess whether relation-conditioned 
memory improves longer-horizon predictions. Dropout labels supplied with the dataset will not be used 
as input because their meaning depends on subsequent behavior. Forecasting activity demand can 
inform analysis of learning participation, but this retrospective experiment cannot establish that a 
teaching intervention improves learning outcomes. 
Trade analysis will retain the native tgbn-trade task: predicting the next annual distribution of 
agricultural trade across partners [20]. A normalized nonnegative readout will convert the shared 
temporal representation into partner scores, with the benchmark's NDCG@10 evaluator used for 
comparison. Historical partner shares and persistence will be essential baselines. Case studies will 
inspect whether coordinated updates track changing partner concentration without excessive turnover in 
stable relationships. The limited number of annual observations makes this a complementary 
distribution-prediction study rather than evidence for fine-timescale responsiveness. Uncertainty will be 
reported conservatively; correlated country records and repeated random seeds will not be treated as 
independent additional years.

Recommendation analysis will use tgbn-genre to evaluate future user–genre preferences under the 
official node-property protocol [20]. The same graph and memory modules will receive typed 
interaction histories and feed a distribution head; normalization and training loss will be appropriate to 
the benchmark labels. Historical preference frequency, recent preference frequency, and compatible 
temporal graph baselines will test whether adaptive memory adds value beyond simple persistence. The 
analysis will separate highly active and infrequent users and examine preference shifts observable from 
past behavior. This extension evaluates the usefulness of the learned representation for recommendation 
ranking, rather than converting recommendation scores into count-forecast errors. 
Transaction analysis will use tgbl-coin for destination ranking from past address-to-address interactions, 
with tgbl-wiki-v2 used first to validate the event-scoring implementation on a smaller public benchmark 
[21]. A pairwise head will score source and candidate destination states at the query time. The 
benchmark supplies the query setting; predicting which destination is selected does not itself predict 
when an event occurs. Every query will be scored before its edge is inserted into memory, and all 
methods will use the same official evaluation candidates and time ordering. MRR, new-partner 
performance, and cost per scored query will be reported. The financial-domain case study will concern 
evolving transaction relationships, without interpreting ranking accuracy as investment performance. 
Task adaptation will preserve the distinction between recorded interaction structure and the sparse 
graph used for message passing. The activity tasks use a continuous nonnegative forecast head, node-
distribution tasks use a normalized score head, and event-ranking tasks use a source–destination head. 
Losses and cached residuals will be task-specific: count residuals become available after an interval 
closes, while ranking or distribution feedback is usable only when its label is released. Time-based and 
activity-based triggers remain available before feedback arrives. The fixed-node approximation analysis 
in Section 4.5 will not automatically be claimed for entity arrivals, changing output spaces, or ranking 
metrics; those extensions require separate assumptions or empirical evidence. 
Each application report will include the public data source, chronological split, task-specific baselines, 
a full-versus-selective replay comparison, a matched update-budget sweep, and representative 
successful and failed episodes selected by a predeclared rule. Plots will show forecast or ranking quality 
alongside graph changes, memory retention, and actual update decisions. Large datasets will first be 
profiled on a predefined time prefix for development; any sampled study will be identified as a subset 
experiment, and official benchmark claims will require the complete prescribed test. Five-seed core 
studies take priority over broad shallow coverage. A final comparison will relate benefits to event 
density, relationship persistence, missingness, and timescale, yielding practical guidance about which 
domains justify adaptive computation. 
Application case selection will be fixed before inspecting final model advantages. For each domain, the 
report will show a typical stable episode, a high-change episode identified from permitted past statistics, 
and the worst predefined evaluation slice. Communication and education reports will emphasize 
workload and participation trajectories; trade will show partner-share shifts; recommendation and 
transaction reports will show ranking changes and new-partner cases. Each example will include a 
strong baseline and the matched uncoupled or ungated control. Explanations will be checked by 
removing the highlighted relationship or retention signal and rerunning the prediction, treating the 
resulting change as model sensitivity rather than proof of a real-world causal effect. 
6 Feasibility Risks and Research Integrity 
My previous graph-learning and spiking-network work provides a foundation in representation learning, 
model optimization, comparative evaluation, and scientific writing. Experience with P-SGCN supports 
the design and analysis of graph-based models, while prior work on event gating and temporal 
integration informs selective computation. The doctoral contribution will require substantial further 

=== PAGE 16 ===
16 
development in dynamic relationship learning, coordinated memory, conditional error analysis, and 
reproducible evaluation across distinct digital interaction domains. 
Implementation will proceed from a small, runnable prototype. I will first reproduce a simple baseline 
and a strong domain model, then add graph learning, memory coordination, and scheduling 
incrementally. Initial trials will use a single benchmark, modest hidden dimensions, and short histories. 
A pilot of at most twelve configurations per new component, using one seed, will identify viable 
settings; shortlisted configurations will then receive multiseed evaluation. Actual runtime and memory 
measurements will determine the full experimental schedule. The planning target is execution on a 
single available accelerator where feasible, with CPU use for small synthetic tests; no particular local 
accelerator is assumed to be available. 
Several failure modes have clear responses. If candidate restriction misses new dependencies, periodic 
exploration will be strengthened and its cost reassessed. If graph learning mainly follows noise, 
smoothing and confidence estimation will be simplified before more modules are added. If spiking 
memory offers no independent advantage, a continuous-state control can establish which part of the 
adaptation mechanism remains useful. If sparse kernels are slower than dense execution, grouped 
updates or coarser scheduling will be tested, and runtime claims will be narrowed if the limitation 
persists. 
The most consequential risk is that the coordination mechanism duplicates emerging work or fails 
against strong independent adaptation. A maintained literature comparison and matched ablations will 
address this early. Negative evidence will guide simpler explanations and clearly bounded contributions. 
Theory will be stated under explicit assumptions, with experiments testing their empirical relevance. 
Synthetic generating edges, recorded interactions, and learned predictive relationships will remain 
distinct. Cross-domain variation will be analyzed as evidence about the method's limits rather than 
averaged away. 
All application studies will use publicly released datasets under their stated research and redistribution 
conditions. Anonymized identifiers will be used only to construct the required temporal relationships, 
without attempting re-identification. Educational and communication predictions will describe 
aggregate recorded activity, while transaction analysis will concern interaction patterns. Historical 
predictive accuracy will not be interpreted as evidence that a policy intervention would improve 
outcomes. Reproducibility materials will provide lawful download instructions and derived protocols, 
with raw data redistributed only where permitted.
